# Source Credibility Rubric

Use this to grade sources before relying on them for a load-bearing claim. Not every
source needs a formal grade, but any claim central to the findings should trace to at
least one source you'd rate Tier 1 or 2 below, or to multiple Tier 3 sources agreeing.

## Tiers

**Tier 1 — Primary / authoritative**
- Peer-reviewed papers (arXiv preprints included, if the field treats them as such),
  official standards documents, primary datasets, official vendor documentation, direct
  government/regulatory filings, an organization's own published data about itself.
- Treat as strong evidence on its own, but still check publish date for time-sensitive
  claims.

**Tier 2 — Reputable secondary**
- Established journalism with editorial standards, well-known technical publications,
  official blog posts from the entity being discussed, conference proceedings.
- Good corroboration; fine as sole support for non-controversial claims, but pair with
  Tier 1 for anything load-bearing or contested.

**Tier 3 — Community / aggregator**
- Forums, Q&A sites, wikis with open editing, marketing pages, SEO-optimized "listicle"
  content, most blog posts without clear authorship/expertise.
- Useful for discovering leads and terminology, not for citing as proof. If a claim only
  has Tier 3 support, say so explicitly rather than presenting it with unwarranted
  confidence.

**Reject / do not cite**
- Content that only restates another source without adding information (circular
  citation — verify you're not citing an aggregator that itself scraped the real source,
  and cite the real source instead).
- Sources with no discoverable author, date, or original claim of authority, presented as
  fact.
- AI-generated content presented as a primary source (it may still be useful as a lead to
  find the real source it's paraphrasing).

## Practical checks

- **Who wrote it, and are they positioned to know this?** An author's own paper about
  their own method outranks a third party's summary of it, but may also be more biased
  about its merits — read critically either way.
- **When was it published/updated?** For fast-moving topics (software versions, prices,
  current events), a Tier 1 source from 3 years ago can be less useful than a Tier 2
  source from last week. State the date either way.
- **Does it cite its own sources?** A source that shows its own evidence trail is more
  trustworthy than one making bare assertions.
- **Is it trying to sell you something?** Vendor claims about their own product's
  performance need independent corroboration before being repeated as fact.
- **Does independent replication exist?** For scientific/technical claims, a result
  repeated by an unrelated group is stronger than a single paper's self-reported numbers.
